Most shipping arguments we see don't start at the port. They start weeks earlier, on a quotation, with three letters nobody looked at closely.

A buyer in Nairobi thinks "CIF Mombasa" means the seller looks after everything. A supplier in Guangzhou quotes "FOB" for air cargo. A trader in Deira agrees to "EXW" and then finds out the buyer's agent can't clear the goods for export from the UAE. Same pattern every time: someone assumed the term meant something it doesn't.

Those three letters are Incoterms. If you buy or sell goods across borders, it's worth ten minutes to understand them properly. This guide is for exactly that.

## What Incoterms actually are

Incoterms are a set of trade rules published by the [International Chamber of Commerce (ICC)](https://iccwbo.org/resources/rules-of-trade/incoterms-2020). The current version is **Incoterms 2020**, and it has 11 rules.

Each rule answers three practical questions:

1. **Where does the seller's job end?** In other words, the point where the goods count as "delivered".
2. **Who pays for which part of the journey?** That covers loading, export clearance, main freight, insurance, unloading and import duties.
3. **When does the risk move from seller to buyer?** If the goods are damaged or lost after this point, it's the buyer's problem.

What they don't cover matters just as much. Incoterms don't decide when ownership of the goods transfers, how or when you get paid, or what happens if one side breaks the contract. The US International Trade Administration's [Know Your Incoterms guide](https://www.trade.gov/know-your-incoterms) makes the same point: they're one part of a sales contract, not the whole thing.

## The 11 rules, in plain English

The rules come in two groups. Seven work for any mode of transport, including air, road, rail, sea and combinations of these. Four are only for sea and inland waterway.

### Rules for any mode of transport

- **EXW (Ex Works)**: The seller makes the goods available at their own premises, and that's it. The buyer arranges loading, export clearance, freight, import clearance and everything else. It's the least work for the seller and the most for the buyer.
- **FCA (Free Carrier)**: The seller hands the goods, cleared for export, to the buyer's carrier at a named place. That might be the seller's warehouse, the forwarder's warehouse or an airport cargo terminal. For most container and air shipments, this is the rule people should be using instead of FOB.
- **CPT (Carriage Paid To)**: The seller pays the freight to the named destination. Risk passes to the buyer much earlier, when the goods are handed to the first carrier at origin.
- **CIP (Carriage and Insurance Paid To)**: The same as CPT, but the seller also buys insurance. Under the 2020 rules, that insurance has to be the higher level of cover (Institute Cargo Clauses A).
- **DAP (Delivered at Place)**: The seller gets the goods to the named destination, ready to unload. The buyer unloads, clears import customs and pays duties and taxes.
- **DPU (Delivered at Place Unloaded)**: The same as DAP, except the seller also unloads the goods at destination. It replaced the old DAT rule.
- **DDP (Delivered Duty Paid)**: The seller does everything, including clearing import customs and paying the buyer's duties and taxes. It's the most work for the seller, and often the hardest to carry out.

### Rules for sea and inland waterway only

- **FAS (Free Alongside Ship)**: The seller places the goods beside the vessel at the named port of loading. This is mostly used for bulk and breakbulk cargo.
- **FOB (Free on Board)**: The seller loads the goods on board the vessel at the named port of loading. Risk passes once they're on board.
- **CFR (Cost and Freight)**: The seller pays the sea freight to the destination port. Risk still passes when the goods are loaded at origin.
- **CIF (Cost, Insurance and Freight)**: The same as CFR, but the seller also insures the cargo. Here the minimum cover is lower (Institute Cargo Clauses C), so check the policy if the cargo is valuable.

## The one thing people get wrong about C terms

With CFR, CIF, CPT and CIP, **the seller pays the freight, but the buyer carries the risk during the voyage.** The cost and the risk split at different points.

So if you buy CIF Mombasa and the container is damaged at sea, the seller has done their job. You, the buyer, make the claim on the insurance the seller arranged. That's why it matters what level of cover was bought, and in whose name.

D terms are different. With DAP, DPU and DDP, the seller carries the risk all the way to the destination.

## How to identify the Incoterm on your documents

A properly written Incoterm always has three parts:

**\[Three-letter rule\] + \[named place\] + Incoterms 2020**

For example: *FOB Jebel Ali, Incoterms 2020* or *DAP Kampala warehouse, Incoterms 2020*.

Here's where to look, and what to check.

### 1. The quotation or proforma invoice

This is where the term should first appear. It's usually next to the price, as in "USD 18,400 CIF Mombasa". If the price has no term next to it, ask before you agree to anything. A price without an Incoterm doesn't tell you what you're paying for.

### 2. The commercial invoice

The commercial invoice should repeat the same term and named place as the quote. If the quote says FOB and the invoice says CFR, stop and fix it before the goods ship. Customs, banks and insurers will all go by the invoice.

### 3. The letter of credit or payment terms

If you're paying by letter of credit, the bank will state the Incoterm and expect the documents to match it. A CIF letter of credit, for instance, will ask for an insurance certificate. If the documents don't match, payment can be delayed.

### 4. The bill of lading or air waybill

The transport document won't usually name the Incoterm, but it gives you a clue:

- **"Freight prepaid"** usually means the seller paid the main freight, which points to a C or D term.
- **"Freight collect"** usually means the buyer pays the main freight, which points to an E or F term.

If the invoice says CIF but the bill of lading says freight collect, something is wrong. Find out before the cargo arrives and someone gets a surprise bill.

### 5. Ask three quick questions

If the documents are unclear, these three questions usually sort it out:

1. **Who is booking and paying the main freight?** If it's the buyer, the term is probably EXW, FCA, FAS or FOB. If it's the seller, it's probably a C or D term.
2. **Where exactly does the seller's responsibility end?** That gives you the named place.
3. **Who is clearing customs at destination?** If the seller is clearing and paying duties, it's DDP. In almost every other case, the buyer handles import clearance.

## Mistakes we see all the time

**Using FOB for containers or air freight.** FOB was written for cargo loaded directly onto a ship. Containers are usually handed over at a terminal days before loading, and FOB doesn't fit air cargo at all. FCA is the better rule in both cases.

**Using EXW when the buyer can't clear exports.** Under EXW, the buyer is responsible for export clearance in the seller's country. A buyer in Lagos usually can't clear goods for export from Dubai themselves. In practice, the seller ends up doing it anyway, outside the agreed terms. If you're the seller and you plan to handle export clearance, FCA reflects what's really happening.

**Agreeing to DDP into a market you don't know.** DDP means the seller pays import duties and taxes in the buyer's country, and usually needs someone there who can clear the goods. Into many markets, that's hard to do without a local partner. Unless you know exactly how clearance works at the destination, DAP is usually safer.

**Vague named places.** "FOB China" or "CIF Africa" means nothing. Name the actual port, airport or address, such as "FCA Dubai South warehouse" or "CIF Dar es Salaam". The more precise the place, the fewer arguments later.

**Leaving out the year.** Older versions of the rules still exist, and they're slightly different. Write "Incoterms 2020" so everyone works from the same version.

**Forgetting about insurance.** Only CIF and CIP oblige anyone to insure the cargo. Under every other rule, it's up to you. If no one arranges cover, the cargo travels uninsured.

## Which Incoterm should you use?

There's no single right answer, but here's a rough guide for traders working out of the UAE:

- **You're buying from China or India and want control of the freight.** Use FCA or FOB at the origin port, and let your own forwarder handle the main leg. You'll usually get better rates and better visibility.
- **You're selling from Dubai to a buyer who has their own forwarder.** FCA at your warehouse or the port keeps your job simple and clear.
- **You're selling to a buyer who wants a delivered price.** CPT, CIP or DAP to their city lets you quote one number while the buyer still handles import clearance.
- **You're shipping to a market you know well, with a trusted clearing agent there.** DDP can work, and buyers like it. Just make sure you've priced the duties correctly.

## Is a new version coming?

Yes, but not soon. The ICC's [2026 workplan](https://iccwbo.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2026/01/2026_ICC_Global-Policy-Commissions_Workplan.pdf) includes starting work on a revision that leads towards Incoterms 2030, and no text or launch date has been announced yet. Until then, Incoterms 2020 is the version to use in your contracts.
